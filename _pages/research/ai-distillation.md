## Abstract

Knowledge distillation lets a developer train a smaller “student” model on the outputs of a larger “teacher” model, capturing much of the teacher's capability at a fraction of its cost. When the teacher belongs to someone else, distillation raises a question existing law answers poorly: can a frontier developer stop competitors from building on its model's outputs? This article argues that no single legal instrument settles the question, and that regulation is better understood through Lawrence Lessig's four modalities — law, contract, norms, and architecture — working together.

Part I explains how distillation works and why it matters, distinguishing lost revenue from a genuine collapse of incentives to invest in frontier models. Part II examines the legal levers. Copyright protection for model outputs is weak, because AI-generated content faces serious authorship obstacles; contractual prohibitions in terms of service are more promising, though their enforceability against click-wrap users and non-parties is contested, and some circumvention is better framed as fraud than as a privity problem. Part III turns to the non-legal modalities: community norms among developers, and architectural measures such as rate limits, output watermarking, and tiered access pricing that make large-scale extraction costly. The article concludes by proposing — rather than claiming to prove — a layered framework in which these modalities compensate for one another's gaps.

**Keywords:** knowledge distillation; AI regulation; Lessig's four modalities; copyright; contract and terms of service; technical protection measures
