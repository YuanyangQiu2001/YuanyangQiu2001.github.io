## Abstract

Under a productive "code/architecture" cyberspace, identification is a central mechanism in the production of digital identity. If an information processor's decision to identify is treated as an economic judgment made by a rational actor under marginal cost constraints, then wherever the marginal profit of identification is positive, the processor has a positive incentive to link the information to a specific natural person — and that information is, in a microeconomic sense, "identifiable." Combined with contextual reasoning, an economic model of the identifiability standard helps courts delimit the scope of "personal information" in hard cases. A judge may first ask whether any identification benefit exists, given the content, nature, and collection context of the information at issue; then examine whether the processor's actual scale of processing reaches a relevant threshold; and finally apply the economic model to determine whether marginal identification profit is positive.
