## Abstract

Roughly thirty states have now enacted statutes governing minors' use of social media, and courts have enjoined a great deal of it on First Amendment grounds. This paper asks how lawmakers can reach the harm without reaching the speech, and gives one organizing answer: **regulate the code, not the content.**

Compulsive adolescent use is engineered on two layers. The content layer — cheap, repetitive, machine-generated "slop" — is the bait. The code layer is the hook: autoplay, continuous feeds with no end marker, variable-ratio reinforcement, and platform-timed push notifications. Only the second is constitutionally tractable. Content-layer regulation fails three times over. *Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Association* bars courts from inventing new categories of unprotected speech however harmful the state believes them to be; strict scrutiny and the least-restrictive-means inquiry have historically defeated child-protection mandates; and *Moody v. NetChoice* protects the compiling and ranking of third-party posts as editorial discretion.

Design features are different because they say nothing. They operate identically whatever the platform has chosen to display, which places them within the content-neutral Time, Place, and Manner framework of *Ward v. Rock Against Racism* and the intermediate scrutiny *Turner Broadcasting* applies to privately owned media: compliance is verified by inspecting a setting rather than reading a post, no post is removed or demoted, and only the default changes. The paper answers two objections. *Moody* protects the platform's choice of what to show — it did not decide whether a feed that simply follows a user's clicks is an editorial judgment at all. And private ownership protects a platform from being compelled to carry speech it dislikes; it does not exempt it from neutral rules about how content is delivered, as *Ward* and *Turner* both make clear.

Tested against current law, the divide holds. Access-based and content-based statutes have consistently been enjoined; design-based statutes are the only category with wins — the Eleventh Circuit allowed Florida's law to take effect on the ground that it regulates how platforms work rather than what users say, and a district court upheld Arkansas's overnight-notification curfew as a valid manner restriction while striking the same statute's ban on "addictive" design as void for vagueness. Texas's HB 18, which polices the feed, and California's SB 976, which resets defaults, illustrate the same contrast. The paper closes with four drafting principles: define the target by feature rather than by psychological effect; define covered platforms by content-neutral criteria; regulate design rather than access; and leave editorial output untouched.
