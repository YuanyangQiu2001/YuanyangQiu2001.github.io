## Abstract

Cross-border data flows are governed today by three competing systems, and their divergence is not merely technical. Comparing the legal texts, policy instruments, and enforcement practices of China, the United States, and the European Union, this paper argues that each is advancing a distinct conception of sovereignty in the digital domain. The EU governs through a rights-based paradigm, exporting its standards via adequacy decisions and extraterritorial judicial rulings. China governs through security-centered territoriality, using data classification and export assessment to keep critical data within its borders. The United States governs through its firms, converting corporate control of data into extraterritorial jurisdiction under the CLOUD Act while deploying investment review selectively to protect domestic industry. The paper traces how these models collide — regulatory fragmentation, competing extraterritorial claims, and the instrumentalization of data rules as levers of geopolitical pressure — and identifies technical standards, cross-border legal authority, and digital infrastructure as the principal sites of contestation. Technologies such as homomorphic encryption and decentralized networks offer partial workarounds, but their development is itself shaped by great-power competition, and existing multilateral instruments remain non-binding. The paper concludes that data governance is no longer primarily a question of technical compliance: it has become a central arena in which states contest power and attempt to reshape the international order.
