## Abstract

Roughly thirty states have now enacted statutes governing minors' use of social media, and courts have enjoined a great deal of it on First Amendment grounds. This paper asks how lawmakers can reach the harm without reaching the speech, and gives one organizing answer: regulate the code, not the content. Content-layer regulation runs into *Brown v. Entertainment Merchants Association*, strict scrutiny, and *Moody v. NetChoice*. Design features — autoplay, endless feeds, variable-ratio reinforcement, platform-timed notifications — operate identically whatever the platform displays, which places them within the content-neutral framework of *Ward v. Rock Against Racism* and *Turner Broadcasting*. Tested against current litigation, design-based statutes are the only category with wins. The paper closes with four drafting principles: define the target by feature rather than by psychological effect; define covered platforms by content-neutral criteria; regulate design rather than access; and leave editorial output untouched.

**Keywords:** First Amendment; teenager social media addiction; content-neutral regulation; platform design regulation; algorithmic accountability; free speech
