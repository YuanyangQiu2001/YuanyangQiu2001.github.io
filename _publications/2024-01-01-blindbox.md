## Abstract

China's blind box market has expanded rapidly since 2016, and with it a set of legal problems that existing regulation addresses only in part. This article argues that the risks of blind box retailing are stratified by type of operator. Small, informal sellers produce overt illegality with concentrated harm — gambling-adjacent sales models and non-compliant contents that can cross into administrative or criminal liability. Large, well-resourced firms produce covert non-compliance with diffuse harm: practices at the margins of legality, including designs that induce compulsive purchasing among minors and disclosure practices that shade into misrepresentation, whose aggregate effect far exceeds that of small sellers because of scale. Building on the State Administration for Market Regulation's 2023 Guidelines for Regulating Blind Box Sales, the article proposes rules in two areas. For the protection of minors, it develops a tiered scheme keyed to the capacity categories of the Civil Code, together with specified identity-verification, spending-limit, and complaint-handling duties for online platforms. For misrepresentation, it defines the "material facts" that must be disclosed — both those enumerated by law and those inferable from ordinary commercial expectations — and argues for reversing the burden of proof, since operators alone hold the probability data and production records on which any consumer claim would depend. The article concludes by proposing a public-private regulatory model combining operator self-regulation, credit-based supervision by regulators, and coordination through industry associations.

**Keywords:** blind box retailing; consumer protection; minors' protection; misrepresentation; burden of proof; platform regulation
