## Abstract

In the modern information society, internet platforms have become the principal channel through which information circulates. A platform's power to manage user accounts rests on a dual chain of authorization: bottom-up, through the disposal rights users cede in terms-of-service agreements, and top-down, through successive delegations of authority in law. It is therefore both civil and administrative in character. In *Xia v. Shanghai [X] Information Technology Co.*, the Huangpu District People's Court of Shanghai considered for the first time whether a social media platform may lawfully prevent a user from deactivating an account the platform has banned. The plaintiff grounded the claim in the right to erasure of personal information; the defendant argued that it was enforcing a policy designed to stop banned accounts from being "reincarnated" through re-registration. The court held that the platform's handling of the account was reasonable, and that refusing deactivation was a necessary measure for discharging its statutory duties. The case opens onto broader questions — how the right to erasure should be limited, how platform "private power" should be regulated, and how the property value of an account should be balanced against the regulatory management of accounts — offering both judicial reference and a research agenda for communication-law problems of platform governance and personal information protection.
